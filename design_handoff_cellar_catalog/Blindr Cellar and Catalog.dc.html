Cellar and catalog · complete redesign

A cellar is a collection. It should read like one.

Cellar and catalog together, because they are one object seen twice: the catalog is the shared record of a wine, the cellar is your bottles of it, and the single wine page is where they meet.

Two things belong in front: what you own — how many, from where, from whom, how varied — and what it tasted like, both your score and the community’s. Everything else steps back. Drink windows keep working and stop being displayed: they are self-entered and personal, so they should not occupy the most visible slot in all 142 rows. Prices and valuation come out entirely and return as their own piece of work, because doing them well is harder than a number in a corner. Both views you like are kept.

Every icon here is a stand-in. The sidebar and header are drawn only to place the cellar in context, and the glyphs inside the mocks are hand-made approximations. The app uses lucide-react and already has its own set — cellar-bottles-table.tsx imports Wine, Star, Search, ChevronsUpDown, ArrowUp, ArrowDown, ChevronLeft, ChevronRight, ExternalLink, NotebookPen, Pencil, Plus, List and LayoutGrid; cellar-summary.tsx imports Wine, Boxes, Coins, MapPin and CalendarCheck. Use those and port none of these paths.

C0

What is there, and what it spends its space on

Read from cellar/page.tsx, cellar-bottles-table.tsx, stats.ts and the cellar migrations. The parts worth keeping are genuinely good — the two views, the append-only consumption log, the import, the visibility model. What follows is what the frame around them puts first.

Space spent on the wrong things
· Three of the four KPI tiles are inventory trivia and the fourth is a valuation. Unique wines, total bottles, estimated value, ready to drink — nothing about what the collection is. · The most prominent cell in every row is a drink window. A coloured chip plus a year range, on 142 rows, for a field the owner typed themselves and which means different things to different drinkers. It is the least reliable fact on the row and it is the loudest. · Money is shown with no way to make it honest. stats.ts silently drops any lot in another currency and sets a mixedCurrency flag the UI never explains, so the total is a partial sum presented as a fact. · Six controls above the table, permanently, none saying how many bottles they would yield. On a phone the control bar is taller than the first row. · The bottle view paginates every 8, which is a slideshow, not browsing. · Drinking a bottle is a page navigation to /cellar/[lotId]/drink, and coming back loses your filter, your page and your place. · History is a flat list with no grouping, no totals, and no route back to the tasting a bottle was poured at.
Ready to use, and unused
· Community scores are one join away. catalog_wine_ratings gives avg_score and note_count per wine and the catalog already renders it beside qualityBand(). wset_notes is readable by every authenticated user — the policy is literally using (true). The cellar queries only your own notes. · Your own note is computed and then hidden behind a Show note button in one of seven columns. The page already resolves your best-scoring note per wine, its id and its date, and NoteModal already opens it in place. · The collection’s shape is half-computed. stats.ts builds bottles by region, colour and decade, then shows them as a bar list in a tab behind two clicks. Producers and grapes — the two that say most about a collector — are not aggregated at all, though the rows carry both. · How much of a lot you have drunk. purchased_quantity is frozen at purchase while quantity falls, so every lot knows “3 of 6 gone”. Nothing renders it. · Which bottles are in tonight’s flight. wine_pour_intents ties a lot to a glass, owner-only, and draws it down at Start — so a bottle committed to tonight still reads as available. · Where a bottle was drunk. The pour writes the tasting name into occasion; History prints it as dead text. · And one thing that does not exist: storage_location is a single free-text field. No racks, no bins, no shelves — so “rack B” in the add-wine mockups was fiction. Treated here as a label you can group by.
Step 1 · Bottles

The list, rebuilt around what you own

Same rows, a frame that leads with the collection and its scores. Both views share this frame — the dimensions, the search, the grouping and the filters apply to whichever is showing.

C1 The cellar, list view DesktopManaging bottles.

The frame now answers “what do I own?” in its first two lines. The KPI tiles become the collection’s dimensions — countries, regions, producers, grapes, vintages — and each is a way into the list: tap one and the list folds by it. The default is ungrouped, as drawn here, because that is the view that can afford to say the most per row: producer above the wine, then grape, colour and designation, then appellation with region and country, and in the columns the size, how much of the lot is gone, where it is, when it came in, and both ratings. Grouping is a dropdown you opt into (C3), and each header then absorbs whatever it made redundant. This is the cellar as you land on it: no filter, no grouping, everything you own in one list, which is why the rows here span Piedmont, Tuscany, Rheinhessen, Veneto and Sicily. C2 and C3 show the same list once a filter or a grouping has been applied. One Ratings column replaces the drink window and the value. Two numbers, no labels on the rows — the header says which is which and the colours hold once you have seen them: your score in bordeaux, the community average in gold behind a star. A dash means you have not tasted it. The sample size is not here; it belongs where there is room to read it, on the card (C2) and on the wine itself (W2). Your score still opens your note. A bottle at its last one says so where the count is. A wine you have not tasted says so and offers Rate it, which is the backlog worth nagging about. Six permanent controls become four: search, Group, Sort, and one Filter carrying a count, with country, region, colour and grape inside it and shown as removable chips. Actions move onto the row on hover — Drink, Rate, and a menu — so no column is reserved for buttons unused on 141 of 142 rows. The row still shows 3 of 6 drunk from purchased_quantity and a gold marker when a bottle is committed to tonight through wine_pour_intents.
No drink window and no price anywhere on this screen. Both stay in the data; the window is editable in the lot sheet (C4) and nothing reads price until valuation is designed properly.

Step 2 · The same rows, looked at differently

Bottles, and folding the list

You like both views, so both stay and neither is secondary. What changes is that they share one frame and one set of filters, and that a long cellar folds into groups instead of being paged through.

C2 The cellar, bottle view DesktopBrowsing.

The bottle stays the content — a tall contained image over cream, which the current card already gets right. Four changes. Both scores become the card’s foot — yours on the left, the community average on the right, each labelled — so a wall of bottles can be read for quality and for whether you have written anything. The meta line carries producer, appellation and region, since a card has no columns to lean on. The count is a corner badge and says last one when it is, because that changes whether you open it tonight. The action row appears on hover only — three buttons on every card means twenty-four competing with eight bottles. And the page holds 24, not 8. The image corner is now free of a readiness chip, which is what it should be: a photograph of a bottle, not a dashboard.

C3 Grouped DesktopSix ways to fold 142 bottles.

Grouping is opt-in: the list is ungrouped until you choose a dimension, either from the five tiles at the top of C1 or from this dropdown — one control, nine values, no chip rail competing with the filters beside it. Grouping applies to both renders — the switch sits where it does on C1 and C2, and grouped bottle cards get the same headers. Rather than hide that, this view surfaces near-duplicates and offers to merge them — the cheapest possible version of tidy storage, with no schema and no migration.

Step 3 · Acting on a bottle

One sheet instead of two pages

Everything you can do to a bottle happens without leaving the cellar — so your filter, your page and your place survive the thing you came to do.

C4 One lot, everything about it DesktopReplaces two pages.

Today a row leads to the catalog wine, and acting on the bottle means /cellar/[lotId]/drink or /edit — two navigations away, and coming back loses your filter, your page and your place. This sheet is both pages plus what neither shows. The two scores lead: yours with its date, its completeness and a way straight into the note, and the community average with its spread and how many of the notes came from friends — an average of 33 with a 86-to-97 range says something a single number cannot. The lot’s own history is here, with the tasting each bottle was poured at as a link; the name is already in occasion, written there by the pour. And this is the one place the drink window appears — quiet, at the bottom, editable, because it has to live somewhere and this is where you would change it.
No price anywhere: price_per_bottle and purchase_source keep being written by the add-wine sheet and the import, and nothing reads them until valuation is designed.

Step 4 · The other two sections

History, and the collection

Bottles, History and The collection become three linkable sub-pages under Cellar rather than client-side tabs.

C6 History DesktopGrouped, counted, linked.

The same consumption rows, given what a flat list cannot give them. Totals for the year — bottles, and the split between tastings, home and gifts, which is the shape of how you actually drink. Months with a subtotal. The occasion as a link: draw_down_flight_cellar_lots writes the tasting name into occasion when a flight is poured, so most rows already know which evening they belong to and currently print it as dead text. And the note column is the backlog: 42 written up, 22 not, with the gap as a filter — the most useful thing history can tell you is which bottles you drank and never wrote down. Money is gone from here too; a year of drinking measured in DKK was the least interesting reading of it.
The privacy line is stated, not implied: cellar_consumptions has no read policy beyond the owner, so a PUBLIC cellar still never exposes what you drank.

Doing valuation properly means handling currency (nothing is converted today — lots bought in EUR would silently drop out of any total), what a bottle is worth against what it cost, and where the estimate comes from. What changes

· The four KPI tiles become the collection’s five dimensions — countries, regions, producers, grapes, vintages — and each one sets the grouping. · One Ratings column replaces the drink window and the value, carrying yours and the community average together. Yours links into NoteModal; the community average comes from catalog_wine_ratings with its note count always beside it. · Drink windows stop being displayed. drink_from, drink_to and readiness() all stay; the window is visible and editable in the lot sheet only, and the readiness bars leave the stats page. · Prices and valuation come out entirely and become a stated coming panel. Writes are untouched: the add-wine sheet, the CellarTracker import and wine-price-field keep storing prices; nothing reads them. · Six permanent controls become four, with country, region, colour and grape inside one Filter and shown as removable chips. · Group by is the navigation model — where it is, producer, region, grape, vintage, colour. Client-side over rows already loaded. · Row and card actions move to hover; the phone opens the lot sheet instead. · The bottle grid holds 24, not 8, and its image corner carries no chip. · The lot sheet replaces /drink and /edit as pages. Keep the routes for deep links; render the sheet at them. · History gains totals, months, tasting links and a without a note filter. · Stats becomes The collection, adding producers and grapes, and your average against the community’s. · Three sub-pages under Cellar — Bottles, History, The collection — instead of client tabs, so a cellar view is linkable. ?tab= redirects.

Kept, needed, and yours to decide

· Kept as-is: the append-only consumption log and its four reasons, frozen purchased_quantity, the CellarTracker import, the visibility control and its read-only cellar, NoteModal, and the persisted view choice through safe-storage. · The country flag stays. <CountryFlag> renders before the region on every row and card today; these mocks print “Piedmont, Italy” as plain text only because flags are not worth hand-drawing. · The community rating needs a join, not a build. catalog_wine_ratings gives avg_score and note_count per wine; catalog/page.tsx already reads it. The spread (highest, lowest, how many by friends) in C4 is more than that view exposes — either widen it or drop the spread. · Needs a query the page does not run: the “in tonight’s flight” marker. wine_pour_intents is owner-readable and joins a lot to a glass, so this is a join. It matters — without it a bottle committed to tonight reads as available. · Needs two new aggregates: bottles by producer and by grape, both the shape of byRegion. · Decide: does storage_location stay free text? Grouping and merging (C3) make it useful with no migration. Structuring it is a much larger build and only pays off if your bottles really are in numbered positions. My read: keep the text, add the merge. · Decide: should drinking offer to write the note immediately, or log the bottle and leave it to the without a note backlog? C5 offers it as a checked default. · Decide: what “your score” is when you have several notes on a wine. The page currently takes your highest. Most recent is the more honest default for a cellar, and the sheet shows the date either way. W2 shows the case: two notes, 88 blind and 86 open. · Decide: does the retail price stay on the wine page? W2 keeps it, because estimated_price is the catalog’s own shop reference with its caveat already written, not a valuation of your bottles — that is the distinction that lets it live here while the cellar’s money is deferred. If you would rather no price appear anywhere until valuation is designed, it comes out of this card too. · The catalog needs no new backend. catalog_wine_usage already returns holders, bottles, lots, notes, appearances and consumptions; fetchWineGuessStats, fetchWineDescriptors, fetchWineStructure and fetchWineBlend supply the rest. The gold cellar strip in W2 is the only addition: your own lots for this wine, which is a query the page does not currently make. · Not drawn: the empty cellar, someone else’s cellar read-only (the readOnly path exists and would now show community ratings but not yours), the import screens, and bulk selection.
